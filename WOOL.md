# Sheep's Wool Insulation for BioDomes

A report on using Portuguese waste sheep wool as natural insulation in BioDome construction, with emphasis on local sourcing, processing, and integration with corkcrete wall and roof systems.

---

## Why Sheep's Wool?

Sheep's wool is one of the highest-performing natural insulation materials available, with thermal properties rivaling synthetic alternatives. In Portugal specifically, it has an unusual advantage: it's a **waste product**.

Portuguese sheep breeds (Churra Mirandesa, Serra da Estrela) produce coarse wool unsuitable for modern textiles. Farmers currently pay to dispose of it or simply discard it. This means raw wool can potentially be sourced **extremely cheaply or free** from local farmers in northern and central Portugal.

### Performance Summary

| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Thermal conductivity | 0.033-0.038 W/mK |
| R-value per 100mm | ~3.5-3.8 |
| R-value per inch | 3.6 (batts) to 4.3 (loose fill) |
| Density | 15-60 kg/m³ |
| Moisture absorption | Up to 35% of weight without losing thermal performance |
| Fire auto-ignition temperature | 560-600°C (twice that of wood) |
| Fire classification | B1-C (EN 13501-1), self-extinguishing |
| Acoustic performance | Excellent (fibrous structure absorbs sound) |
| Nitrogen content | ~14% (natural flame resistance) |

Wool's standout property is **hygroscopic moisture management** — it absorbs and releases water vapor without losing insulation performance. No synthetic insulation can do this.

## Sourcing in Portugal

### The Waste Wool Opportunity

A student at the **Polytechnic Institute of Braganca** developed a project highlighting that Portuguese sheep wool is treated as waste with no commercial value. The project (related to the AbrigoVivo initiative) proposes repurposing this waste wool as thermal and acoustic insulation — turning an agricultural disposal problem into a construction resource.

### Where to Source

| Region | Breeds | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Tras-os-Montes (Braganca, Miranda do Douro) | Churra Mirandesa | Coarse wool, abundant, farmers eager to dispose |
| Serra da Estrela | Bordaleira Serra da Estrela | Used historically for Burel fabric; leftovers available |
| Alentejo | Merino Branco, Campaniça | Finer wool but still largely unsold |
| Centro (Guarda, Viseu) | Various | Mixed flocks, contact local cooperatives |

**Contact points:** Local agricultural cooperatives, farmers' markets, municipal veterinary offices (they track flock numbers), or Burel Factory in Manteigas (which has offered wool leftovers for insulation prototypes).

### Estimated Cost

| Scenario | Cost |
|----------|------|
| Direct from farmer (raw, unwashed) | Free to €0.50/kg |
| From cooperative (bulk, unwashed) | €0.50-1.50/kg |
| Washed and carded (processed) | €3-6/kg |
| Commercial wool insulation batts (import) | €15-30/m² at 100mm |

DIY processing makes the most economic sense for a BioDome project.

## DIY Wool Processing

Raw wool from the farm needs processing before use as insulation. The steps are straightforward but labor-intensive.

### 1. Skirting and Sorting

Remove heavily soiled sections (belly wool, leg wool, dung tags). These go to compost, not insulation. Keep the cleaner fleece from the back and sides.

### 2. Washing (Scouring)

Raw wool contains lanolin (grease) and dirt that must be removed:

1. Fill large tubs or a bathtub with hot water (50-60°C)
2. Add a small amount of detergent or soda ash
3. Submerge wool, gently press — do not agitate or it will felt
4. Soak 20-30 minutes
5. Drain and repeat with clean hot water (2-3 rinses)
6. Final rinse in cool water
7. Squeeze out excess water gently — do not wring
8. Spread on racks or mesh to dry in sun and wind

### 3. Carding (Optional but Recommended)

Carding opens up the wool fibers and creates a uniform loft:

- Hand carders work for small batches
- A drum carder speeds up the process significantly
- The goal is fluffy, even batts — not yarn-quality preparation

For loose-fill insulation (stuffing into cavities), rough carding or simply teasing apart by hand is sufficient.

### 4. Borax Treatment (Essential)

Borax (sodium tetraborate) provides moth resistance, fire retardancy, and mold inhibition:

1. Dissolve borax in warm water: **8-10% solution** (80-100g borax per liter of water)
2. Submerge washed wool in the borax solution for 15-20 minutes
3. Squeeze out excess — do not rinse
4. Dry thoroughly on racks

The borax remains in the wool fibers permanently, providing ongoing protection. It is non-toxic to humans at these concentrations.

> **Borax is critical.** Untreated wool will attract moths that destroy the insulation over time. Borax also raises the wool's fire resistance and inhibits mold growth in damp conditions. Do not skip this step.

## Comparison: Wool vs Corkcrete

| Property | Sheep's Wool | Corkcrete (cork-hemp-lime) |
|----------|-------------|---------------------------|
| Thermal conductivity | 0.033-0.038 W/mK | 0.065-0.080 W/mK |
| R-value per 100mm | ~3.5-3.8 | ~1.4-1.5 |
| Density | 15-60 kg/m³ | 280-350 kg/m³ |
| Fire rating | B1-C (self-extinguishing) | B1 (lime is non-combustible) |
| Moisture absorption | Up to 35% of weight, retains R-value | Low (cork repels, lime buffers) |
| Vapor permeability | Very high | Very high |
| Structural capacity | None (batt/loose fill only) | Low-moderate (monolithic wall) |
| Installation | Friction-fit or loose fill, immediate | Wet-mixed, packed, 2-5 week cure |
| Pest risk | Moths (needs borax treatment) | None |
| Cost (DIY sourced, PT) | Near-free to €3-6/kg processed | €7-11/m² at 100mm |
| Embodied carbon | Very low | Very low (carbon negative) |

**Key difference:** Wool is pure insulation with ~2.5x better R-value per thickness. Corkcrete is a structural insulation composite — it fills wall cavities, bears some load, and insulates in one material. They serve different roles and complement each other.

## Application in BioDome Roof Assembly

### Proposed Roof Layering (inside to outside)

The BioDome uses a wattle dome structure — interwoven eucalyptus poles forming a curved basket-weave. The roof layering must work with this irregular, curved surface.

| Layer | Material | Thickness | Purpose |
|-------|----------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | Eucalyptus wattle dome | Structural | Load-bearing structure |
| 2 | Corkcrete infill (Balanced mix) | 50-75mm | Fills wattle gaps, creates continuous surface, base insulation |
| 3 | Corkcrete insulation (Insulation mix) | 50-75mm | Primary thermal layer |
| 4 | Sheep's wool padding | 25-50mm | Thermal boost, cushion/protection for EPDM |
| 5 | EPDM membrane | 1mm | Waterproofing |

### Why Wool Works as Sub-EPDM Padding

- **Thermal boost:** 25-50mm of wool adds R-0.9 to R-1.9 on top of the corkcrete
- **Cushion:** Protects EPDM from any rough corkcrete texture
- **Conformable:** Wool drapes over the dome curvature naturally
- **Low weight:** At 15-60 kg/m³, negligible load addition
- **Near-free material** if sourced locally as waste wool

### Condensation Management

EPDM is a vapor barrier — moisture that reaches the wool layer can only escape downward. This is the primary risk.

**Mitigation strategy:**

1. The **corkcrete layer beneath is vapor-open** — moisture can dry downward through it into the interior
2. A thick enough corkcrete layer (100mm+) keeps the **dew point inside the corkcrete**, not at the wool
3. **Borax-treated wool** resists mold even if it gets seasonally damp
4. Wool absorbs **35% of its weight** in moisture without losing thermal performance — it can handle seasonal wetting and drying cycles
5. Good **interior ventilation** (see moisture management practices) keeps indoor humidity low, reducing condensation drive

### Alternative: All-Corkcrete (No Wool)

If condensation risk is a concern, the roof can be built without wool:

| Layer | Material | Thickness | R-value |
|-------|----------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | Eucalyptus wattle dome | Structural | — |
| 2 | Corkcrete infill | 50-75mm | 0.7-1.1 |
| 3 | Corkcrete insulation | 75-100mm | 1.1-1.5 |
| 4 | Lime skim coat | 5-10mm | — |
| 5 | EPDM membrane | 1mm | — |

This is simpler and eliminates moisture concerns entirely, at the cost of lower total roof R-value (~1.8-2.6 vs ~2.7-4.5 with wool).

## Application in BioDome Walls

Wool is best used as a **targeted upgrade** rather than whole-wall insulation in BioDomes:

### North Wall Thermal Boost

The north-facing wall receives no direct sun and is the primary source of winter heat loss. Adding a 50-75mm wool layer on the interior face of the north wall provides a significant R-value boost where it matters most.

**Installation:**
1. Complete the corkcrete wall infill and allow it to cure
2. Attach light timber battens (25mm) vertically to create a service cavity
3. Stuff borax-treated wool between battens
4. Cover with breathable fabric or light timber boarding
5. Apply lime render or clay plaster over the boarding

### Cost Estimate (North Wall Only)

For a ~8m² north-facing wall section at 75mm wool thickness:

| Item | Cost |
|------|------|
| Raw wool (DIY processed) | €5-15 |
| Borax | €3-5 |
| Battens and fixings | €15-25 |
| **Total** | **€23-45** |

Negligible cost for a meaningful comfort improvement.

---

## Recommendation

**Use both materials in their ideal roles:**

- **Corkcrete** as the primary wall infill and roof base layer — structural, moisture-resistant, affordable
- **Sheep's wool** as a targeted thermal upgrade — north wall interior, sub-EPDM roof padding

The combination delivers better overall performance than either material alone, using two waste/local resources (discarded wool + abundant cork) at minimal cost. This aligns with the BioDome philosophy of turning local problems (invasive eucalyptus, waste wool, undervalued cork) into building resources.
